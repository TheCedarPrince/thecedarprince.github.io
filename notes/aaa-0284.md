</p></div><p class="noindent"><span class="cmbx-10">Summary: </span>Some overviews of causality from different fields
</p><p class="noindent">This is just a quick stubbed out note and loose grouping to try and define what is
referred to as “causality”.
</p><p class="noindent">Basically, there are many different flavors of causality and it is not clear what is
meant by them. It confuses me to no end the types of causality that exist – not from
a strictly philosophical or quantitative dimension but even in colloquial speech. In my
mind, the most accessible and common way of talking about causality is using order
theory:
</p><p class="noindent"></p><div class="math-display"><img alt="A ≤ B
" class="math-display" src="aaa-0284-e1cbabc13849ed38063523ac21487c1e.svg"></img></div><p class="noindent">Which could be said in multiple different ways as “<span class="cmmi-10">A </span>before <span class="cmmi-10">B</span>”, “<span class="cmmi-10">B </span>after <span class="cmmi-10">A</span>”, or “<span class="cmmi-10">A</span>
precedes <span class="cmmi-10">B</span>”. All it requires is that there is an order between two things. What this
order operator, <span class="cmsy-10">≤</span>, is exactly and what <span class="cmmi-10">A </span>and <span class="cmmi-10">B </span>are do not really matter so much to
me.
</p><p class="noindent">My friend Oscar Smith and I have spoken about this in the context of differential
equations and dynamical systems modelling but I haven’t had much a chance to dig
deeper into it. Moreover, Nate Osgood and I have also reflected on the fact that
causality can also mean something extremely different in the social and health
                                                                  

                                                                  
sciences. And even still in quantum mathematics, there is a whole slew of literature
about causality. To me, it seems like every field has a particular subtlety with their
definition.
